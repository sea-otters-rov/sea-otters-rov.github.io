🌊 The one rule that beats all others

Teams don't lose because their robot is bad. They lose because they under-practice piloting and treat documentation as a last-minute chore. Build early, then spend your remaining time driving and writing — not gold-plating hardware.

Design fundamentals

Frame: start cheap, upgrade later

Every veteran gives the same progression. Don't skip steps.

MaterialWhenWhy
½" PVC pipeYear one, alwaysCheap, sold everywhere, cut with a hand tool, forgiving of mistakes. Every beginner kit uses it.
HDPE sheetLevel-2 upgradeDoesn't absorb water, resists cracking, can be cut into a lighter hydrodynamic shape.
Aluminium extrusionAdvanced, sparinglyStrong & stiff — but conducts electricity, corrodes when scratched, and is heavy. Veterans use it for rails and camera arms, not the whole frame.

Rule of thumb: master waterproofing and thruster placement on a PVC frame first. Graduate to HDPE/aluminium once you understand what actually breaks.

Thrusters: don't over-engineer year one

  • Simple layout (start here): 4 horizontal thrusters (forward / reverse / yaw / strafe) + 2 vertical (up / down). Easy to wire, easy to reason about in code.
  • Vectored layout: thrusters at 45° let fewer motors produce combined vectors — but you need mixing math you can debug under time pressure. Save it for year two.
Rookie call

A simple 4+2 arrangement you understand completely beats a vectored layout you can't fix when it misbehaves 10 minutes before your run.

Buoyancy: tune it, don't calculate it

The most-cited community advice on buoyancy is refreshingly un-mathematical:

  • "It's easier to add weight than to add floats." Build the frame slightly over-buoyant on purpose, then trim down with ballast.
  • Floats on top, weights on the bottom. Keeps the ROV self-righting instead of tumbling. (Center of gravity low, center of buoyancy above it.)
  • Aim slightly positive, not perfectly neutral. If you lose power or the tether snags, the vehicle rises safely instead of sinking into the silt.
  • Tune empirically: float the ROV in a tub with a mesh bag of washers/BBs, add or remove weight until it sits right. Split weight evenly left/right and front/back — uneven ballast is the #1 cause of an ROV that lists to one side.
Foam trap

Pool noodles compress and waterlog — your ROV will sink lower by the end of a pool session. Use rigid XPS "blue/pink board" insulation foam (~$6 a sheet) for anything past the first prototype. Plain white bead foam (EPS) also soaks up water — avoid it.

Waterproofing: the vacuum test is non-negotiable

  • Use tubes, not boxes. Cylindrical / domed enclosures spread water pressure evenly. That's why every commercial pod is a tube.
  • Vacuum-test every enclosure before every dive. Pull a vacuum, watch the gauge. If it doesn't hold on dry land, it will flood underwater. Test the empty enclosure submerged before you trust it with your Raspberry Pi.
  • Cheap DIY connectors (~$6): a PEX faucet swivel + PVC riser, cable through stepped heat-shrink, cavity potted in epoxy — field-tested to ~40 m. Fully embed solder joints in epoxy so water can't wick down the strands.
  • Beware "IP68" labels. Treat any IP68 servo/camera as "probably fine in a pool for 15 minutes," not "guaranteed" — pot and seal it yourself as backup.
  • Stop galvanic corrosion. Stainless bolts against aluminium eat the aluminium in a wet environment. Use Tef-Gel or dielectric grease on every dissimilar-metal joint.

Tether: the invisible anchor

A tether that's too heavy sinks and drags the ROV backward and down; too buoyant and your vertical thrusters fight the cable instead of moving the vehicle. Aim for neutral-to-slightly-positive buoyancy — add small foam floats along flat Cat5 cable, or bundle it with floating polypropylene line. Many "underpowered" ROVs are just fighting their own cable the whole run.

⚠ Never tug the tether

Pulling the tether to speed up recovery is a rule penalty, not a technique. Your tether manager pays out and reels in slack smoothly — never hauls the ROV in by the cable.

Electronics & control

Three architectures show up again and again. Pick the lowest tier that meets your ambitions — you can always level up.

Thrusters & motors

  • Commercial: Blue Robotics T100/T200 + one Basic ESC each. The T200 is the default competition choice — even at half throttle it out-muscles a bilge-pump thruster.
  • Budget DIY: convert a 500–750 GPH submersible bilge-pump cartridge (~$15–20) into a thruster and drive it with a brushed-motor RC ESC. A converted pump pulls ~4 A at 12 V — worth it for the thrust.
  • Keep ESCs dry and onboard, close to the thrusters, to minimise voltage drop over a long tether.
Fuse & power rules the judges check
  • One blade fuse (ATO/MINI) in the positive line, within 30 cm of the power connector.
  • Measure your actual full-load amps in water, then pick the next standard fuse size up — don't guess from datasheets.
  • Run one thick power pair down the tether (not thin per-thruster wires) to cut resistive losses. For 25 A over 50 ft, 8 AWG keeps voltage drop tolerable.

Cameras & control

  • Mount the main camera in the clear dome for a wide view; add a cheap second camera aimed at your claw for close-up task work.
  • Standard USB gamepads (Xbox/PS) are the norm — nearly every open-source stack supports them. Good Python starting points: piborg/Gamepad and Footleg/pygame-controller.
  • Analog cameras = lower latency & cheap; digital/HD = sharper for judges. For slow precision work, image clarity usually beats the last few milliseconds of latency.

Build cheatsheet

Buy-once tool kit

  • Temperature-controlled soldering iron + solder + wick
  • Digital multimeter (your most-used tool)
  • Heat gun for heat-shrink
  • Wire strippers, flush cutters, crimpers, needle-nose pliers
  • PVC pipe cutter (cleaner than a hacksaw)
  • Hot glue gun, electrical tape, zip ties
  • Screwdriver sets (6-in-1 + jeweler's)
  • Budget: $50–100 covers a genuinely usable starter kit

Prototype in this order

  1. One waterproof thruster + ESC in a bucket — prove you can spin and control a motor underwater before building anything else.
  2. The empty watertight enclosure — vacuum-test dry, then submerge it empty. Confirm the seal before trusting it with electronics.
  3. A 2-thruster "test mule" — validate your control code and gamepad mapping on a throwaway frame.
  4. Buoyancy on the bare frame — learn how much foam and ballast you actually need before final assembly.
Pool-day habits
  • Re-run the vacuum test before every session — seals degrade.
  • Power off before you solder or probe a live circuit. Shorting "while just fixing one wire" fries control boards.
  • Rehearse the real mission tasks against a clock, not just "driving around."

Team roles

MATE requires at least 3 students + a mentor, run like a company. Borrow the control-room structure real subsea operations use:

Role 01

Pilot

Primary hands on the main controller. Owns vehicle movement and task execution. Stays locked on the main camera feed.

Role 02

Co-pilot

Runs the manipulator, aux cameras, and checklists. Calls out position and orientation so the pilot never has to look away.

Role 03

Tether manager

Pays out and reels in slack smoothly, keeps the cable clear of obstacles — and never pulls the ROV in by the tether.

Rotate roles in practice. A team that depends on one irreplaceable pilot is fragile the day that student is sick — or graduates.

Practice & strategy

  • Rehearse real tasks against the clock. Pool time is for mission rehearsal, not joyriding.
  • Debrief every run: what you designed, how the run went, how you communicated under pressure, how you reacted when something broke. (The free E-Compete tool structures this.)
  • Know when the clock stops. A mission isn't done until the ROV is back at the surface and in a teammate's hands. Practice a fast, rehearsed retrieval.
  • Plan task order to minimise up/down transits — you don't have to surface between tasks.
Documentation is 40% of your score

The engineering & communication deliverables — technical documentation, presentation, marketing, safety — are worth roughly 40% of the total. Start your technical report at the start of the season, not the night before.

Read past winning technical reports (MATE archives them) to calibrate what "good" looks like. And note: the 2026 rules ban AI-written documentation — the writing must be yours.

Mistakes that sink teams

  • Over-trusting "waterproof" ratings — always vacuum-test your own enclosures.
  • Building the real buoyancy plan around pool noodles that waterlog mid-season.
  • Ignoring tether drag until competition day.
  • Mixed-metal connector corrosion — no Tef-Gel / dielectric grease.
  • Soldering a live circuit "just to touch up one joint."
  • Weeks on hardware, one session of piloting practice.
  • Treating the technical report as a night-before writeup.
  • Never fully reading the 70+ page rules — losing avoidable penalty points.
  • No mentor with real hands-on electrical/mechanical experience.

What MATE ROV really is

MATE isn't just "build a robot." Your team acts like a small ocean-engineering company that has been hired to solve real underwater problems. You design and build a remotely operated vehicle — an underwater robot connected to the surface by a cable (the tether) — then pilot it through timed missions in a pool while judges evaluate your engineering, your safety, your documentation, and your teamwork.

It's part robotics, part startup, part sport. And crucially: you win as much on land as in the water. Roughly half your score comes from how well you explain and present your work.

How a season flows

A rough timeline from "we're a team" to competition day:

Step 1 · Fall

Choose your class

SCOUT, NAVIGATOR, or RANGER — this sets your power budget, your rules, and your ambition. See The Classes.

Step 2 · Fall–Winter

Read the mission & design

Study the year's tasks, then design an ROV and tools to tackle them. Start your project notebook now — it becomes your report.

Step 3 · Winter

Build & waterproof

Frame, thrusters, electronics, buoyancy. Test each subsystem alone before combining. See the Build Guide.

Step 4 · Spring

Practice & document

Rehearse the real tasks against a clock; write the technical report and prepare the presentation and poster in parallel.

Step 5 · Late spring

Pass safety, compete

Clear safety inspection, run the mission, present to judges. Regional winners in RANGER advance to the World Championship.

Our goal

From 4th to the podium

We know the flow now. This season is about executing it better — especially the paperwork.

You're a company, not just a build team

MATE deliberately frames everything in industry terms, and it shapes how you're scored:

  • Your team is a company; the pool run is a product demonstration for a client.
  • You produce a technical report, deliver an engineering presentation, and design a marketing poster.
  • Students do all the work — mentors only advise, and judges deduct points if adults clearly did the building or writing.
  • You even file a real safety analysis (JSEA) in RANGER, just like an offshore operator would.

The vocabulary

Terms you'll meet everywhere — learn them once:

TermWhat it means
ROVRemotely Operated Vehicle — your underwater robot, controlled from the surface.
TetherThe cable carrying power and signal from the surface to the ROV.
Product demoThe timed pool run where you complete mission tasks.
Payload / manipulatorThe tools and claw the ROV uses to do tasks.
ThrusterA motor + propeller that moves the ROV.
BuoyancyWhether the ROV floats, sinks, or hovers. You tune it with foam and weights.
SIDSystem Interconnection Diagram — your wiring schematic (must show the fuse).
FLAFull Load Amps — the current your ROV draws with everything running, measured in water.
JSEAJob Safety & Environmental Analysis — a required safety document (RANGER).
FloatA separate, self-diving profiler device you build for one mission task.

Your first month

  1. Pick your class and read its Manual Rules + Specs (see Resources).
  2. Read the 2026 mission and list which tasks look achievable. See The Mission.
  3. Start a shared project notebook — log every decision, sketch, and test from day one.
  4. Assign roles: pilot, co-pilot, tether manager, plus documentation and outreach leads.
  5. Build a test-mule thruster in a bucket to learn waterproofing and control before committing to a full design.
  6. Skim the Rookie Playbook so you don't repeat the mistakes that sink new teams.
One mindset that wins

Build early so you have time to practice piloting and write your documentation well. Those two things — not a fancier robot — are what separate 4th place from 1st.

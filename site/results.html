Why this matters

Most rookie teams pour everything into the robot and treat the documents as homework. We did the opposite of what hurts you: we built a genuinely fast, capable ROV. Now we just have to collect the points we're already allowed to have on paper.

What the judges wrote

Straight from the two score sheets:

  • “A very beautiful marketing display, but it's missing a lot of the why and the how.”
  • “Need to explain better how the ROV ties to the actual mission.”
  • “Theme should address MATE challenges.”
  • “Need to cite sources for photos and include captions.”
  • “Sponsorship should be acknowledgements.”

Add up just the Abstract, Company Evaluation, and a solid Theme section, and that's more than the 12.2 points between us and 3rd — from three text boxes we simply didn't fill in.

What the judges actually asked us

Our Engineering Presentation delivery was a real strength — C-Stars earned “Exceeds Requirement” for both Delivery and Teamwork. Where we wobbled was the deep technical follow-ups. The judges' own notes list the questions they probed with:

  • Which Raspberry Pi? Which Python libraries, and why Python? What runs the camera?
  • Who designed the 3D-printed thruster shrouds? Where did the ROV enclosure come from?
  • What did the claw prototyping phase look like? How did you land on the thruster layout?
  • How did you test for buoyancy? Why should someone buy your ROV?
The note that stings — and helps

On one Fins sheet: the judge ran out of time on Q&A, and wrote there was “evidence that the students weren't 100% sure what was happening.” The fix is simple and free: every member owns a subsystem they can explain cold, and the whole team rehearses these exact questions before the event.

The two-team trap

Here's the sharpest lesson of the whole weekend. One judge scored The Fins' presentation far lower than the others and explained why in plain language:

Judge's overall comment · The Fins

“SC06 and SC18 had almost the exact same design in all aspects. Main differences were paint. The design might work and get points in the product demo, but the same design doesn't net points for understanding and unique design.”

Two teams from the same school turned up with what looked like the same robot in different colors. That quietly cost us twice:

  • Unique-design points vanished. “Unique ROV design” is a discretionary bucket — identical builds can't both claim it.
  • The “adults built it” flag. Every judge marked a deduction for suspecting mentors/parents built the vehicle or wrote the presentation — and one judge marked it “Extreme,” dropping that presentation to 17. Two matching robots make that suspicion worse, not better.
The fix

If we run two teams again, they build genuinely different vehicles — different frame, different tooling, different rationale — and each documents its own decisions. Different paint is not a different design.

Seven lessons for next year

None of these are about building a better robot. We already do that. They're about capturing the points we're already allowed to have.

Lesson 01

The binder wins podiums

Our pool run was top-3 in the region. The spread between teams lives in the paperwork. Give the documents the same hours we give the robot.

Lesson 02

Claim the free points

Abstract, Company Evaluation, Acknowledgments (thank MATE by name), photo captions and credits, labeled section headers, cited sources. We left more here than the gap to 3rd.

Lesson 03

Answer “why” and “how”

“Missing the why and the how.” Every design choice needs a reason tied to a mission task — not just a label on a part.

Lesson 04

Tie it to the theme

Connect the ROV to real-world problems and the MATE challenges, and cite your sources. This one section was worth 7 points; we scored 1.

Lesson 05

Two teams ≠ two paint jobs

If we field two teams, build two genuinely different robots with their own rationale. Identical designs forfeit unique-design points and invite the “adults built it” deduction.

Lesson 06

Prove the kids built it

Log the build with photos and notes all season. Each member owns a subsystem and can explain the code, buoyancy, claw, and thruster layout on demand.

Lesson 07

Everyone rehearses the Q&A

Don't run out of time and don't look unsure. Write down the exact questions the judges asked this year (they're right above), assign them, and drill them before the event.

The one-sentence takeaway

We're already a podium-level team in the water. Next year we just have to be a podium-level company on paper.

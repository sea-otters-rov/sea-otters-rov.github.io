Build the vehicle

Your ROV is six subsystems working together: a frame, propulsion, buoyancy, a waterproof brain, a tether, and a manipulator. Build each one to a spec, test it alone, then combine. Here's how — and in what order.

Build in this order

Don't build the whole ROV and hope. Prove each risky part in isolation first — it's far cheaper to find a leak in an empty tube than around your Raspberry Pi.

  1. One waterproof thruster + ESC in a bucket — prove you can spin and control a motor underwater.
  2. The empty watertight enclosure — vacuum-test dry, then submerge it empty.
  3. A 2-thruster test mule — validate your control code and gamepad mapping on a throwaway frame.
  4. Buoyancy on the bare frame — learn how much foam and ballast you need before final assembly.
  5. Integrate — only now build the real thing.

Frame

Start cheap. A ½" PVC frame is the right choice for year one — sold everywhere, cut with a hand tool, forgiving of mistakes. Upgrade to HDPE sheet (doesn't absorb water, lighter, more hydrodynamic) once you understand what actually breaks. Use aluminium extrusion sparingly — it's strong but heavy and corrodes.

RANGER weight cap

RANGER vehicles must stay under 25 kg, and lighter earns a bonus (<15 kg = +10 pts). PVC keeps you light; over-building in metal can cost real points. SCOUT and NAVIGATOR have no weight limit but still must be hand-launched.

Thrusters & propulsion

A simple layout you understand beats a clever one you can't debug:

  • Start with 4 horizontal + 2 vertical thrusters — forward/reverse/yaw/strafe plus up/down. Easy to wire and to code.
  • Commercial: Blue Robotics T100/T200 + one Basic ESC each — the default competition choice.
  • Budget DIY: convert a 500–750 GPH bilge-pump cartridge (~$15–20) into a thruster, driven by a brushed-motor RC ESC.
  • Keep ESCs dry and onboard, close to the thrusters, to minimise voltage drop over the tether.
  • Shroud every prop to IP-20 (see Safety) — a finger must not reach the blade.

Buoyancy

Tune it empirically — don't try to calculate the perfect float volume:

  • Build slightly over-buoyant, then trim with ballast. "It's easier to add weight than to add floats."
  • Floats on top, weights on the bottom — keeps the ROV self-righting.
  • Aim slightly positive so the vehicle rises if you lose power or snag the tether.
  • Split ballast evenly front/back and left/right — uneven weight is the #1 cause of an ROV that lists.
  • Use rigid XPS foam, not pool noodles (which waterlog and lose buoyancy mid-session).

Waterproofing

  • Use a tube, not a box — cylindrical enclosures spread water pressure evenly.
  • Vacuum-test before every dive. Pull a vacuum, watch the gauge. If it doesn't hold dry, it floods wet. Test the empty enclosure submerged before trusting it with electronics.
  • Cheap DIY connectors (~$6): a PEX faucet swivel + PVC riser, cable through stepped heat-shrink, cavity potted in epoxy. Fully embed solder joints so water can't wick down the strands.
  • Treat "IP68" parts as "probably fine in a pool for 15 minutes," not guaranteed — pot them yourself.
  • Stop galvanic corrosion with Tef-Gel or dielectric grease on every dissimilar-metal joint.

Tether

The tether carries power and signal to the surface — and can secretly anchor your ROV. Aim for neutral-to-slightly-positive buoyancy: add small foam floats along flat Cat5, or bundle it with floating line. Run one thick power pair (not thin per-thruster wires) to cut voltage drop, and give it real strain relief at both ends. Never pull the tether to recover the ROV — it's a penalty.

  • Control with a standard USB gamepad — nearly every open-source stack supports it (piborg/Gamepad, Footleg/pygame-controller).
  • Cameras: a main camera in the clear dome + a cheap second camera aimed at the claw for close-up task work.

Manipulator (the claw)

You don't need a fancy claw — you need one matched to the mission props. Reading the 2026 tasks reveals the targets:

  • Most grab targets are ½" PVC — design your gripper around a ½"-diameter pipe and it covers coral, basket stars, and sensors.
  • Several docking tasks use a Velcro hook-to-loop connection — a simple funnel or wedge end effector aligns and mates them.
  • Pickups often use a hook and rope — a bent hook beats a complex claw for those.
  • Design one versatile tool that serves several tasks before adding specialised payloads.

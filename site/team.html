Team & Practice

Teams don't lose because their robot is bad. They lose because they under-practice piloting and can't explain their own design to a judge. Build early — then spend the time you saved on driving, rehearsing, and writing.

Deck roles

MATE requires at least 3 students + a mentor, run like a company. Borrow the control-room structure real subsea operations use:

Role 01

Pilot

Primary hands on the main controller. Owns vehicle movement and task execution. Stays locked on the main camera feed.

Role 02

Co-pilot

Runs the manipulator, aux cameras, and checklists. Calls out position and orientation so the pilot never has to look away.

Role 03

Tether manager

Pays out and reels in slack smoothly, keeps the cable clear of obstacles — and never pulls the ROV in by the tether.

Rotate roles in practice. A team that depends on one irreplaceable pilot is fragile the day that student is sick — or graduates. And add documentation and marketing leads early — that's where our podium went last year (see the season plan).

Practice & strategy

  • Rehearse real tasks against the clock. Pool time is for mission rehearsal, not joyriding.
  • Debrief every run: what you designed, how the run went, how you communicated under pressure, how you reacted when something broke. (The free E-Compete tool structures this.)
  • Know when the clock stops. A mission isn't done until the ROV is back at the surface and in a teammate's hands. Practice a fast, rehearsed retrieval.
  • Plan task order to minimise up/down transits — you don't have to surface between tasks.
  • Start the technical report at the start of the season. After every build session, whoever made a decision writes down the why — the report then writes itself. (Why this matters so much: Scoring & Deliverables.)

Judge Q&A rehearsal

In 2026 the judges probed us with specific, answerable questions — and noted when we froze. The full list is on Our 2026 Scorecard; the drill is simple:

  • Every member owns a subsystem — code, buoyancy, claw, thrusters, electronics — and can explain its why and how cold. Deferring to the owner is fine; silence is not.
  • Rehearse the exact questions judges asked last year: which board and libraries, who designed what, how you tested buoyancy, why someone should buy your ROV.
  • Invite an adult to play a mean judge for a mock session before the event.
  • Log the build with photos and notes all season — it's your proof that students, not mentors, did the work. Every judge looks for this.

Mistakes that sink teams

  • Over-trusting "waterproof" ratings — always vacuum-test your own enclosures.
  • Building the real buoyancy plan around pool noodles that waterlog mid-season.
  • Ignoring tether drag until competition day.
  • Mixed-metal connector corrosion — no Tef-Gel / dielectric grease.
  • Soldering a live circuit "just to touch up one joint."
  • Weeks on hardware, one session of piloting practice.
  • Treating the technical report as a night-before writeup.
  • Never fully reading the 70+ page rules — losing avoidable penalty points.
  • No mentor with real hands-on electrical/mechanical experience.
  • Two teams showing up with the same design in different paint — judges notice, and it costs both teams.

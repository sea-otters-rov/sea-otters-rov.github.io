NAVIGATOR vs RANGER

The fly-through videos look almost identical — same pool, same props, same storyline. The difference is in the scoring detail: RANGER adds subtasks, splits some subtasks into harder tiers, and pays more for the same physical motion. This page puts every scored line item next to its counterpart — it's the deep reference behind our class choice, which is settled in the Season Plan 2027.

Both classes deduct 5 points per infraction for tether pulling (after one warning), illegal poolside communication about ROV status, debris left in the pool, and requested diver assistance. Safety violations are 5 points for minor, 10+ for major, and officials can disqualify outright.

Task 1 Coral garden — 50 vs 85

A ½-inch PVC ridge on the bottom, 1–2.5 m long and about 36 cm wide, standing at an unknown height. The garden structure is the same in both classes, but the eight 10 cm coloured squares used as photo targets are only specified in the RANGER manual. RANGER collects two corals instead of one, unlocks a photogrammetry route worth 40, and adds a flying subtask NAVIGATOR doesn't have.

1.1 Collect species — NAV 20 · RANGER 30

Specimens sit on the bottom within 2 m of the garden. "Returning" one means lifting it clear of the water and setting it on the pool deck. NAVIGATOR's specimens weigh under 5 N in water; RANGER's under 15 N, so RANGER needs noticeably more lift.

SubtaskNAVRNWhat you actually do
Two basket stars, 5 each1010Grab an O-ball on a ½-inch PVC base and bring it to the deck. Identical in both classes.
Coral collected by the substrate1020The coral is chenille pipe cleaners stuck into a 2-inch end cap on a PVC base. Full credit only if your gripper touches the cap or pipe and never the pipe cleaners. NAV collects one, RANGER two at 10 each.
Coral collected not by the substrate510Half credit if you grab it anywhere on the fuzzy part. RANGER can mix — one by the substrate, one not.

1.2 Ridge modelling — pick ONE route

Three mutually exclusive routes to the same deliverable: a dimensioned representation of the garden shown to the judge. Only one scores, but you may attempt more than one and take the best result. In every route, a ruler or other object of known size may be placed near the garden to help — just remember to retrieve it or it counts as debris. Height is measured from the bottom of the structure to the top of any tees on it.

Route A · Photogrammetry — RANGER only, up to 40

Fly overlapping photos around the garden, transfer the images to a laptop at the station (by hand is fine), and let software stitch them into a 3D model you can rotate on screen for the judge. The piloting may be manual; the reconstruction must be the program's work.

1.3 Fly a transect — NAV 0 · RANGER 15

RANGER only, and it's really an altitude-control test disguised as a survey.

SubtaskNAVRNWhat you actually do
Fly a transect over the garden on video—15At regionals: fly end to end along a 3 m × 0.5 m pipe rectangle. Two blue pipes must stay in frame the whole way while two red pipes 40 cm outside them must stay out of frame — that pins you to a narrow altitude band. At Worlds it becomes: hold station over a 50 cm blue square for 30 unbroken seconds in a current, without the surrounding red square entering frame.

Task 2 SmartAtlantic — 100 vs 135

The same five subtasks in both classes. RANGER adds a computer-vision tier, a keel-depth measurement, a second threat-level question, and one extra prop.

2.1 Mitigate invasive species — NAV 15 · RANGER 20

A 50 × 50 cm sheet of corrugated plastic underwater, covered in laminated photos of European Green crabs mixed with native Rock and Jonah crabs. You only ever report the green crabs. In RANGER it's one attempt only — no guessing; the NAVIGATOR manual states no such rule.

SubtaskNAVRNWhat you actually do
Count the crabs — by image recognition—15RANGER only, and it's genuinely automated: your program must draw a bounding box around each green crab and print the total on screen. Native crabs must not get bounding boxes and must stay out of the count.
Count the crabs — manually105A team member counts off the video feed and tells the judge. Identical work, worth double in NAVIGATOR.
Upload to the Invasive Species Form55Scan the QR code at the station, submit the count on a phone or laptop, show the judge the confirmation screen.

2.2 Iceberg tracking — NAV 25 · RANGER 35

A PVC-and-bubble-wrap iceberg floating in the pool. Survey it, then do desk work with a map. Distance bands are the same in both classes: red inside 5 nautical miles of a platform, yellow 5–10, green beyond 10. One minute of latitude equals one nautical mile. RANGER adds one override for the surface-platform call: a berg whose keel is 110% or more of the water depth at the platform grounds before arriving and is always green.

SubtaskNAVRNWhat you actually do
Survey all five perimeter numbers1010Five digits are attached to the berg — one at each corner 15 cm below the surface, one on the bottom of an underwater leg. Show each on your video display. Only one to four found drops you to 5.
Measure the keel depth—10RANGER only. A ½-inch PVC leg hangs 0.5–1.5 m down from one corner; measure how deep its tip sits below the surface. 10 within 5 cm, 5 within 5.01–10 cm, nothing beyond.
Threat level to the four surface platforms1510Plot the berg's position and heading against the platform map and call green/yellow/red for each. NAV pays 15 / 10 / 5 for four / three / two correct; RANGER pays 10 for all four, 5 for three, nothing below.
Threat level to the subsea assets—5RANGER only, and all four or nothing. A different question: compare keel depth to water depth for any berg passing within 25 nm. 90% up to 110% is red, 70–90% yellow, and both under 70% and 110%-or-more are green — a deep enough keel grounds before it arrives.

2.3 Whale safe fishing gear — NAV 15 · RANGER 20

A milk-crate lobster pot with a stowed buoy, simulating on-demand ropeless fishing gear. Identical prop, identical actions — RANGER just pays double for the first step.

2.4 Recover the buoy anchor — NAV 15 · RANGER 15

The one subtask that is completely identical between the two classes, down to the hardware.

Task 3 Wind & oil — 30 vs 60

The cleanest split on the whole sheet. NAVIGATOR does half of RANGER's task — the cable job only. The entire micropile installation doesn't exist in NAVIGATOR.

3.1 Micropile installation — NAV 0 · RANGER 30

RANGER only, and unusual: the ROV never carries the micropile. Teammates lower it by hand on a 3.1 m pole while the pilot talks them in over the camera feed.

SubtaskNAVRNWhat you actually do
Place the bubble curtain—10Carry a ring of ½-inch PEX tubing down and drop it so it completely encircles the orange PVC at the base of the target. Must happen before the micropile goes in — afterwards the points are gone.
Guide the micropile into place—10Poolside members lower it blind; you give directions off the camera ("20 cm right, down 50") until it seats inside the 2-inch receiver.
Pull the pin to release it—10Once seated you may finally touch it. Pull the pin out of the two 3/16-inch holes so the lowering rope comes free, then get the rope out of the water. The pin itself counts as debris.
A penalty that only exists in RANGER

Touching the micropile with the ROV before it's installed costs 5 points, up to twice — a 10-point swing. And if the micropile detaches from its rope before it's seated, it's unrecoverable and that part of the task is over.

3.2 Powering the platform — NAV 30 · RANGER 30

Same total, different composition: NAVIGATOR pays double for the pickup and skips the cover; RANGER splits those points across an extra step. RANGER's cable is 4 m to NAVIGATOR's 2 m.

SubtaskNAVRNWhat you actually do
Retrieve the power connector105Lift a PVC connector off the wind-farm subsea platform by its screw hook. Dropping it later doesn't cost the points, but you'll need it back to finish.
Lay the cable through the waypoint1010Vertical PVC posts on the bottom — four in NAVIGATOR, two in RANGER; the trailing cable must end up lying inside them, and stay there. This is a route-planning problem — decide where the cable runs before you pick the connector up.

Two vertical profiles — 40 in both classes

Same headline number, completely different scoring logic. Both classes score each profile separately, so the tables below are per profile and double.

NAVIGATOR · scored on autonomy

A profile is simply surface → bottom → surface. There's no depth-holding and no ice. What you're paid for is how little human involvement there is. Pick the cell that describes your float:

MechanismIndependent (no tether)Autonomous but tetheredManually operated
Buoyancy engine — pumps fluid or air in and out to change density201510
Any other mechanism — e.g. a thruster or water jet15105

"Independent" means onboard batteries and no line of any kind. "Autonomous but tethered" means surface-powered but a program, not a person, decides to reverse and come back up. "Manual" means someone poolside flips the switch or works the pump. Two profiles at the same tier gives 40 at best, 10 at worst.

RANGER · scored on depth control

Independence is assumed — every RANGER float is untethered and battery-powered. The points are in precision: a profile means descending to 2.5 m, parking there, then rising to 40 cm below the ice and parking again. Depth-holding is proven after the fact from the recovered telemetry: seven sequential packets spanning the 30 seconds.

Hold 2.5 m for 30 seconds—5Bottom of the float between 2.27 and 2.83 m. Drift out of band and the 30 seconds restarts from zero.
Hold 40 cm for 30 seconds—5Top of the float between 0.07 and 0.73 m. Tell the judge your sensor offset before you deploy.
Breaking the surface or touching the ice—−5Penalty applied inside the task total, not the penalty box. You may run extra profiles and substitute a better one — but whole profiles only, no mixing the good half of one with the good half of another.

Data — NAV 10 · RANGER 20

SubtaskNAVRNWhat you actually do
Float transmits its data after recovery—10RANGER only. Back on deck, the float wirelessly dumps its log to your receiver. Full credit needs one packet per 5 seconds of both profiles; 5 points if it manages at least one packet from after the descent.
Graph depth over time1010Time on X, depth on Y, axes labelled. NAV graphs data MATE hands out at the start — 10 by computer, 5 by hand. RANGER must graph its own telemetry, on a computer, with at least 20 packets.
The RANGER fallback

If your float never gets built, comes up silent, or logs fewer than 20 packets, ask the judge for MATE's data and graph that for 10 points. It replaces both the "communicates all data packets" 10 and your own graph — capping that block at 10 instead of 20 — but you keep the profile-completion points (the 30-second depth holds can only be proven from your own telemetry) and the packet sent before descending. NAVIGATOR uses MATE-supplied data by default, which makes those 10 points close to guaranteed with a prepared spreadsheet.

What RANGER actually demands

Strip out the point values and the extra capability RANGER asks for is a short list. If your build can already do these, the gap is smaller than it looks:

  1. Photogrammetry — a camera pass good enough to reconstruct 8 targets, plus a scaling workflow. Worth up to 40 in Task 1.
  2. Object detection — bounding boxes and a live count for the crab sample. Worth 15 instead of 5.
  3. Underwater measurement — coral garden height and iceberg keel depth to ±5 cm. Roughly 25 points hang off measurement accuracy.
  4. Depth-holding altitude control — the transect flight demands a stable height over the bottom.
  5. A telemetry float — closed-loop depth hold plus a radio link you designed on both ends.
  6. Weight discipline — 10 real points for staying under 15 kg.
Where RANGER is easier

Three subtasks are worth more in NAVIGATOR for identical work: counting crabs by hand (10 vs 5), retrieving the power connector (10 vs 5), and the surface-platform threat call (15 vs 10). And NAVIGATOR's float can be tethered and manually operated and still score. If you're weighing the jump, that's the honest counterweight.

Manual errata worth knowing

A few places where the 2026 manuals contradict themselves. In every case the score sheet is the document that governs on the day — but check the official rulings thread before relying on any of this.

  • The NAVIGATOR Part 1 manual ends Task 1 with "Total points = 85 points". Its own itemized list and the score sheet both total 50. The 85 is RANGER's number, left in by copy-paste.
  • NAVIGATOR's Mission Scope summary lists "remove the cover from the oil platform port" under Task 3, but no such subtask appears in the NAVIGATOR task breakdown or score sheet. It's a RANGER-only subtask worth 5.
  • NAVIGATOR's eDNA frequency example table lists 8 species while the surrounding text asks for "each of the ten species". RANGER's table genuinely has 10.
  • Both regional score sheets label Task 4 as "3.1" — cosmetic, but don't let it confuse a scorekeeper.
  • The RANGER manual calls the oil-platform power port 1½-inch pipe in one sentence but 2-inch in two others (matching NAVIGATOR). Build for 2-inch.
